The Great Eclipse Quiz

Tonight the Moon slides in front of the Sun and takes a huge bite out of it — about 93% of the Sun disappears over the Algarve, right at sunset. Play the quiz, drag the Moon around, and find out what is actually going on up there.

🚫👁️

Read this bit first — it matters

Never look straight at the Sun. Not for one second, not even when it is mostly covered, not even when it looks dim and orange near the horizon. It can damage your eyes permanently, and it does not hurt while it happens, so you would not know until later.

The only safe way to look is through certified eclipse glasses (ISO 12312-2), with a grown-up nearby. Sunglasses do not work. Nor do smoked glass, exposed film, or a phone camera. The safest option of all is a pinhole projector — you look down at a picture of the Sun on the ground, not up at the Sun.

Part three

What happens tonight, minute by minute

All times are Algarve local time (WEST). They shift by a minute or two between Sagres and Vila Real de Santo António — the Sun reaches the west coast first.

18:33 First contact — the first nibble The edge of the Moon touches the edge of the Sun. Through eclipse glasses you will see a tiny notch, like a mouse took a bite out of a biscuit. Without glasses you would notice nothing at all.
19:00 Half gone About half the Sun is hidden, and it still looks like a totally normal evening. Your eyes are extremely good at adjusting, which is exactly why people underestimate how dangerous staring at it is.
19:38 Maximum — about 93% covered The big moment. The Sun is a thin curved sliver low in the west-northwest, roughly 9 degrees above the horizon — about the width of your fist held out at arm's length. The light turns strange and metallic, shadows go razor-sharp, and it feels a few degrees cooler.
20:00 The Moon starts letting go The sliver thickens again as the Moon slides off. Meanwhile the Sun is sinking towards the sea, so the whole thing is happening inside a sunset.
20:27 Sunset — while still eclipsed The Sun goes down before the Moon has finished crossing. So the last thing the Algarve sees is a bitten-into Sun dropping into the Atlantic. The eclipse technically finishes below the horizon, where nobody here can watch it.

Facing west matters tonight — anything blocking the horizon (a hill, a hotel, a big pine tree) will hide the best part. The west-facing sunset spots and Sagres at the far southwestern corner have the cleanest view of the horizon in the whole Algarve.

Part four

Six things worth knowing

The facts behind the quiz answers — handy if a small person asks you a follow-up question you cannot answer.

🎯

The luckiest coincidence in the sky

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon — and, by pure chance, about 400 times further away. So the two discs look almost exactly the same size from Earth. No other planet in the Solar System gets a match this neat. Total eclipses exist because of a fluke.

📐

Why not one every month?

There is a New Moon every 29 and a half days, so why not an eclipse every month? Because the Moon's orbit is tilted about 5 degrees. Most months the Moon's shadow sails harmlessly above or below Earth and misses completely.

🇪🇸

Totality goes to Spain, not us

The dark inner shadow — the umbra — crosses eastern Greenland, western Iceland and northern Spain tonight, just clipping the northwestern tip of Portugal. The Algarve sits in the lighter outer shadow, the penumbra. That is the whole reason we get 93% instead of 100%.

🌳

Look down, not up

Every gap between leaves works as a tiny pinhole camera. During the eclipse the little dots of light under a tree stop being round and turn into hundreds of identical crescents scattered across the ground. It is free, it is beautiful, and it is completely safe to look at.

🐦

Animals get fooled

During a deep eclipse birds often stop singing and head for their roosts, crickets start up as though it were dusk, and bees head home. Tonight the effect gets blurred because it is nearly sunset anyway — the animals were getting ready for bed regardless.

📅

Then it happens again next year

This is the first totality over mainland Europe since 11 August 1999. Remarkably, the next one is only a year away: on 2 August 2027 the path crosses southern Spain and North Africa, and the Algarve gets another very deep partial. Two in two summers is an absurd bit of luck.

Questions grown-ups ask

Eclipse FAQ

What exact time is the eclipse in the Algarve?

The Moon first touches the Sun at about 18:33 WEST, maximum coverage of roughly 93% happens at about 19:38 WEST, and the Sun sets at about 20:27 WEST while still partly covered. Times vary by a minute or two across the region, so treat these as a guide and be outside a good ten minutes early.

Will it actually go dark here?

No — and this surprises people. Even at 93% the remaining sliver of Sun is still ferociously bright. What you get instead is an odd silvery quality to the light, unusually crisp shadows and a noticeable drop in temperature. Full darkness only happens inside the narrow path of totality, which tonight runs through northern Spain.

Are eclipse glasses really necessary at 93%?

Yes. The danger does not scale down gently with coverage — the exposed sliver is still direct, unfiltered sunlight, and the retina has no pain receptors to warn you. Use only glasses certified to ISO 12312-2, check them for scratches or pinholes first, and discard any that are damaged. If you have no glasses, use a pinhole projector instead: two sheets of card, a pin, and you watch the image rather than the Sun.

Can I photograph it with my phone?

Pointing an unfiltered phone or camera at the Sun can damage the sensor, and lining up the shot tempts you into looking directly at it. If you want a picture, photograph what is happening around you instead: the crescent-shaped light under the trees, the strange colour of the light, everyone's faces. Those are better photographs anyway.

Where in the Algarve should we watch from?

Anywhere with a clear, unobstructed view towards the west-northwest, because the Sun sits only around 9 degrees above the horizon at maximum. West-facing beaches and clifftops beat inland gardens for this. Sagres and the southwestern coast have the most open horizon; further east the coastline curves and the view is still fine, just aim for height and no buildings in the way.

Is this the same as a lunar eclipse?

No, they are opposites. In a solar eclipse the Moon passes between us and the Sun and blocks it, which is why it is dangerous to look at. In a lunar eclipse the Earth's shadow falls on the full Moon, turning it coppery red — that one is completely safe to watch with bare eyes, and there happens to be one later this month on 28 August 2026.

And for the grown-ups

Maximum eclipse lands at 19:38 and the Sun sets at 20:27 — which is, not coincidentally, exactly when the Algarve's west-facing terraces are at their best. If you are planning to watch from somewhere with a drink in your hand, we have opinions about where.
